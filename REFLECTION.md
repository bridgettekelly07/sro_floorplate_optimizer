## What the source turned out to be

- The two 50% figures are two tests, not one ratio stated twice. This only became clear when the tool was
  run on a partial conversion (Case 2) and passed one while failing the other. *Prompt: when did you
  notice this, and what did you go back to the documents to check?*
- "An average of 200 SF … will be considered" is discretionary. The tool ended up reporting both readings
  rather than choosing, and the strict one turned out to roughly halve what the stock can produce. *Prompt:
  how did you decide to present a discretion rather than resolve it?*
- The compensation question has no single answer in the sources: which five of ten tenants leave is left to
  the permit process. The right output was a range with a reason. *Prompt: what did it take to accept that
  a tool can answer "it depends, and here is on what"?*

## What building the interface revealed

- Every plan the tool draws from a footprint is a type, not a survey. Making that visible on the plan
  ("only 10 of 13 rooms fit inside the outline") mattered more than making the plan prettier.
- The abstract trade-off curve was accurate and unreadable; the map coloured by the scenario, a bar of
  where the tenants go, and a phase timeline replaced it. *Prompt: what did you learn about the difference
  between a correct representation and a legible one?*
- The district's ordering rule had a blind spot found by reasoning, reproduced in a test, and fixed with a
  search; the search then found a better order on the real stock. *Prompt: what does "evidence" mean for a
  claim the sources do not make?*
